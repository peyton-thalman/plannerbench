PlannerBench · built by Peyton Thalman

People ask AI about retirement. I tested how often it’s right.

AI models are good at one money rule at a time. They slip when several rules meet in one household, and most don’t notice when the facts they were given are wrong. This site shows where, by how much, and what fixes it.

What I found Read the report

Why I made this

I’m a financial advisor, and I meet most people by knocking on their door. More and more of them tell me they get their financial advice from ChatGPT.

So I ask: how do you know it’s right? The usual answer is that they Google it afterwards and read the top result. These days the top result is another AI’s answer. Now two AI models have told them two different things about the same question, and they don’t know which one to trust. Many would still rather ask a chatbot than an advisor.

I wanted to know what the models still get wrong. So I wrote the questions planners work through every week, built an answer key I could prove, and tested the models one by one.

What I built to find out

  • An answer key I could prove. A planning engine: the 2026 tax, Social Security, withdrawal and Medicare rules written as code. A second engine, written separately from the published rules alone, agrees with it on — random households to the dollar.
  • A benchmark. — questions with one exact answer each, so a script does the grading and nobody’s opinion is involved. A newer set adds harder questions and client notes with mistakes planted in them.
  • The engine as a tool. The same engine plugs into Claude or ChatGPT, so the model asks it for the number and explains the result, and has no reason to make one up.

Check it yourself

  • Every number here can be rebuilt. Each model reply is saved, and one command regrades all of them from scratch.
  • The published source decides. When models agreed with each other against the key, the IRS form settled it, never a vote. When a check found a mistake on my side, I fixed it and logged it.
  • It’s a benchmark, so it runs on any model. Part of the question set is held back so answers can’t be memorized. If you’d like a model tested, get in touch.
  • The limits are written down. Small samples, 95% intervals on every score, and a list of what wasn’t tested, in the report and on the method page.
