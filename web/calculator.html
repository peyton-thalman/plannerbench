With tools · AI plus a calculator it can trust

Hand it the right answer, and it argues. Show it the rule, and it listens.

In short

I turned the answer key into a calculator the AI can use while it answers. At first the calculator only gave back the right number, and the AI overruled it with its own wrong rule. Once the calculator also showed the rule and the IRS form line, the AI got every hard question right. On whole-household decisions, every AI I tested got everything right with the calculator, including a cheap one that got 2 of 11 right on its own. And when a key fact was left out, it asked for it before answering. (For the technical reader: the calculator is an MCP server, the standard way to plug tools into AI models.)

Step one · the benchmark’s hardest questions

The same 50 hard questions, asked three ways

First the AI on its own. Then with a calculator that sent back only the answer (tools 1.0). Then with one that sent back the answer, the rule behind it and the IRS form line (tools 1.1). The calculators are general ones (federal tax, Social Security, required withdrawals, Medicare premiums), so the AI still has to choose what to put in and combine what comes out. These are the 50 hardest questions, which is why Claude Haiku scores lower here than its 77% overall.

    Step two · the Gauntlet

    Real retirement decisions, with and without the calculator

    I call this set the Gauntlet. Each question is a whole made-up household (about 16 facts: Social Security, IRAs, a pension, a brokerage account) and a choice people really face: how much to convert to a Roth, when to claim Social Security, how to give to charity, how to empty an inherited IRA. Getting it right means combining at least three rules over 25 years. There are 110 of these; the first run below asked one of each kind plus a trap.
