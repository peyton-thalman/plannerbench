PlannerBench · by Peyton Thalman, financial advisor in Sun City West, Arizona

“It seems right.”

That’s what retirees tell me when I ask how they know ChatGPT’s retirement advice is correct. So I built a test to find out.

The doorstep

I’m a financial advisor, and I build my practice by knocking doors in Sun City West, a retirement community outside Phoenix. Most people tell me they already have an advisor. Then a lot of them tell me they ask ChatGPT their money questions anyway.

So I ask them: how do you know it’s right?

The answer is almost always the same. It seems right. They Google it, the top result agrees, and that settles it. But that top result is usually Google’s own AI summary. One AI is fact-checking another.

I wanted a real answer to my own question, so I built one.

What I built

I built an answer key for real retirement questions: taxes, Social Security, Medicare, and Roth conversions. A planning engine computed it from the 2026 IRS, Social Security, and Medicare rules, and a second program, written independently, checked it. Then I asked AI models the same questions and graded their answers.

I started with Anthropic’s Claude models and some open-source models, then added OpenAI’s, because that’s what most people actually use. Then I tested what happens when the person asking gets their own facts wrong.

What I found

1. One rule at a time, it’s great.

Ask about a single rule, and the best models get it right about — of the time.

2. Several rules at once, it depends heavily on which AI.

Real retirement questions mix taxes, Social Security, and Medicare together. On the hardest of those, the best model scored — and Claude Sonnet 5.5 scored —. The model behind the regular ChatGPT app scored —.* Cheap and free models scored under 30%.

A calculator that only handed over the right number didn’t fix it. A better calculator did. When the calculator showed the rule and the IRS form line behind each number, Claude Haiku went from — to —.

3. It believes whatever you tell it.

I hid one impossible fact in each client file. They were the kind of mistakes people make on doorsteps every day: a wrong birthday, transposed digits, a guess at monthly spending. Most models gave confident advice anyway. Some gave retirement advice to a 14-year-old. Even the best models missed — of the planted errors.

What it means for you

Asking AI is often a good place to start. Then check every age, balance, and income it used, and ask which rule it applied. If it got a fact wrong or can’t name the rule, don’t act on the answer. Take decisions you can’t undo, like when to claim Social Security, to a person.

What it means for firms

Your clients are asking AI before they ask you, even in a town full of retirees. They don’t know when it’s wrong, and the AI won’t tell them when their own facts are wrong. AI can do the math when it’s given the right tools. What’s missing is someone checking the inputs.
