AI gets simple retirement questions right. On real ones, it depends which AI you ask.

The best AI I tested, OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol, got — of my hardest retirement questions right and Claude Sonnet 5.5 got —; — cheap and free models scored under 30%, and three of them almost never noticed a fact that couldn’t be true.

“Best” on this site means right most often and, on wrong facts, catching the most mistakes without false alarms (refusing files that were fine).

Why I made this

I’m a financial advisor, and I meet most people by knocking on their door. My dad became a financial advisor in 1999; watching him take care of people is why I do this work.

More and more people tell me they get financial advice from ChatGPT. When I ask how they know it’s right, they Google it, and the top result is often another AI’s answer. So I wrote the questions planners work through every week, built an answer key I could prove, and tested the AI models people use: Anthropic’s Claude, OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Gemini, and cheaper or free models anyone can download.

The straight answer

Should I trust AI with my retirement questions?

  • For one well-known rule, mostly yes. This year’s tax brackets, or how much you must take out of an IRA (a retirement account you haven’t paid tax on yet): Claude Sonnet 5.5 got — of these right.
  • For your whole situation, it depends a lot on which AI. When taxes, Social Security and Medicare all affect each other, GPT-6.1 Sol got nearly all of my hardest questions right; the cheap and free models got most of them wrong.
  • Check what you type in. In my tests, three cheap models almost never questioned an impossible fact, and even the best two missed — of them. They handed back a confident answer anyway.

Why you can trust the numbers

I don’t have a computer science degree. I directed AI coding agents, mostly Claude Code, to build and run all of it; I decided what to test, what counts as right, when to spend money and what to publish.

That’s exactly why nothing rests on one program. Every answer is computed by two separately written programs that must agree within —, and tax answers also by outside tax software built by other people. A third engine, written by an AI agent that never saw the first, agrees on — random households within —.

Then a model from another lab, OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol, audited the whole project and found — errors. All are fixed and listed in the report. That makes the numbers more trustworthy, not less: someone other than the builder looked hard, and what they found is public.

A CPA (a licensed tax accountant) hasn’t signed off yet. That’s an extra human check on top, not the only one: the independent programs above already agree.

Before you trust an AI with a retirement number: three things

  1. Give it your real numbers, then check what it used. Read back the ages, balances and income in its answer. A wrong fact gets you a confident wrong answer.
  2. For a whole-household decision, use one of the best models, or one connected to a real calculator, and ask it to show the rule. In my tests, the calculator helped most when it showed the rule and its source along with the number.
  3. Take big moves to a professional. When to claim Social Security, a Roth conversion (paying tax now so savings grow tax-free later), anything you can’t undo.
