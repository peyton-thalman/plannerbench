PlannerBench · built by Peyton Thalman

People ask AI about retirement. I tested how often it’s right.

AI is good at simple money questions. It gets real-life ones wrong more often than right, and it usually doesn’t notice when you’ve told it something wrong. I measured all of it and built a fix.

What I found Read the report

Why I made this

I’m a financial advisor, and I meet most people by knocking on their door. More and more of them tell me they get their financial advice from ChatGPT.

So I ask: how do you know it’s right? The usual answer is that they Google it afterwards and read the top result. These days the top result is another AI’s answer. Now two AI models have told them two different things about the same question, and they don’t know which one to trust. Many would still rather ask a chatbot than an advisor.

I wanted to know what the models still get wrong. So I wrote the questions planners work through every week, built an answer key I could prove, and tested the models one by one.

What I found

Four things, in plain English. Click any one for the details.

1

Ask AI one simple question, and it’s almost always right.

—

That’s how many simple questions Claude Sonnet got right. Things like “what’s the tax on this income?” or “how much do I have to take out of my IRA this year?”

 2

Ask it a real-life question, and it gets most of them wrong.

—

Real retirement questions mix taxes, Social Security and Medicare all at once. The same AI got only this many right. On the hardest ones, a wrong answer was usually off by about $1,100 a year.

 3

Give it a calculator it can trust, and it gets them all right.

— → —

I built a calculator that knows every rule. When the AI used it and stopped doing the math in its head, it went from 4 in 10 right to every single one, on 50 hard questions.

 4

Tell it something wrong, and it usually doesn’t notice.

73% vs 1–3%

I slipped one mistake into each client file, some as obvious as a retiree who was 14 years old. The two best AIs caught 73% of them. Three of the cheaper ones caught almost none.

Then a bigger problem: what if you tell it something wrong?

By this point I had shown that AI can get retirement math right, as long as it uses a trusted calculator and doesn’t guess.

But the more people I talked to, the more I saw a different problem. An answer is only as good as what you type in. People mistype an account balance. They guess at their income. They get the year they retired wrong. The AI believes all of it, does perfect math on a wrong number, and hands back an answer that looks just as confident as a right one. Then people make real decisions from it.

So I tested that too. I wrote 30 short client files and put one mistake in each. Some were hard to miss, like a retiree who was supposedly 14 years old, or spending ten times what the family owns. A good advisor would stop and say “wait, that can’t be right.”

The two best AIs stopped about 3 times out of 4. Three of the cheaper ones almost never did: they gave a confident answer more than 8 times out of 10.

What I built to find out

  • An answer key I could prove. To grade the AI, I first needed the right answers. So I wrote the 2026 tax, Social Security, withdrawal and Medicare rules as a program, the planning engine. A second program, written separately from the published rules alone, agrees with it on — random households to the dollar.
  • A test any AI can take. — questions with one exact answer each, so a script does the grading and nobody’s opinion is involved. A newer set adds harder questions and client files with mistakes planted in them.
  • The calculator, for anyone. The same program plugs into Claude or ChatGPT. The AI asks it for the number and explains the result, so it has no reason to make one up.

Check it yourself

  • Every number here can be rebuilt. Each model reply is saved, and one command regrades all of them from scratch.
  • The published source decides. When models agreed with each other against the key, the IRS form settled it, never a vote. When a check found a mistake on my side, I fixed it and logged it.
  • It’s a benchmark, so it runs on any model. Part of the question set is held back so answers can’t be memorized. If you’d like a model tested, get in touch.
  • The limits are written down. Small samples, 95% intervals on every score, and a list of what wasn’t tested, in the report and on the method page.
