PlannerBench · by Peyton Thalman, financial advisor

AI gets simple retirement questions right. On real ones, it depends which AI you ask.

I tested AI models on the retirement questions I work through with families, against answers I could prove. A trusted calculator rescues some models but not others. And many don’t notice when you tell them something wrong, which is now the biggest gap.

Should I trust it? What I found

Why I made this

I’m a financial advisor, and I meet most people by knocking on their door. My dad became a financial advisor in 1999. Watching him take care of people is why I do this work.

More and more people tell me they get their financial advice from ChatGPT. When I ask how they know it’s right, they say they Google it. These days the top result is often another AI’s answer.

So I wrote the questions planners work through every week, built an answer key I could prove, and tested the AI models people use: Anthropic’s Claude, GPT from OpenAI (the company behind ChatGPT), Google’s Gemini, and several cheaper or free models anyone can download.

The straight answer

Should I trust AI with my retirement questions?

  • For one well-known rule, mostly yes. This year’s tax brackets, or how much you must take out of an IRA (a retirement account you haven’t paid tax on yet): the best AIs I tested are almost always right.
  • For your whole situation, it depends a lot on which AI. When taxes, Social Security and Medicare all affect each other, the cheap and free models I tested got most of my hardest questions wrong. The very best got nearly all of them right in my newest test.
  • The biggest risk is what you type in. Many AIs won’t notice when a number you gave them can’t be right, and they’ll hand back a confident answer anyway. Double-check what you tell it, and take big decisions to a professional.

What this means for you

  • Use AI to learn a rule, not to make the decision. It’s good at explaining one rule at a time. It’s much less reliable when your taxes, Social Security and Medicare all affect each other.
  • Check what you typed. A wrong age, balance or income gets you a confident wrong answer. Read your numbers back before you trust the result.
  • The AI you pick matters. On my hardest questions, a free model could be far worse than the best one. For big moves, like when to claim Social Security or a Roth conversion (paying tax now to move savings into an account that’s tax-free later), get a professional to check it.

How I know the answers are right

Grading AI is only fair if the answer key is right. So I wrote the 2026 tax, Social Security, required-withdrawal and Medicare rules as a program, the planning engine, and had it checked by things that didn’t write it.

How this was built

I don’t have a computer science degree. I directed AI coding agents, mostly Claude Code, to build and run all of it. I made the calls: what to test, what counts as right, when to spend money on a run, and what to publish.

Because AI did much of the work, I didn’t want anyone to take it on trust, including me. That’s why the checks above are independent of the agent that built the rest.
