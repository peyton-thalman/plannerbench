Case study

I made the calls. AI agents did the building. Independent checks caught their mistakes.

I’m a licensed financial advisor, not a software engineer. In October 2026 I directed AI coding agents, mostly Claude Code, to build a planning engine, a test of AI on retirement math, and a way to plug the engine into Claude and ChatGPT. In November I added harder questions and the wrong-facts test, then had a model from another lab audit the whole thing. This page covers three things the others don’t: the decisions I made, the mistakes that got caught, and who did what. The results are on the finding pages; the checks themselves are on How I checked.

The problem

“Is AI smart?” is the wrong question

  • The real question. Which planning questions can an advisor trust a model with on its own, and which need verified math behind it? Nobody could answer that with evidence.
  • Why it matters. In this field a confident wrong number costs a family real money: a Roth conversion sized wrong here gave up $26,002. And a firm’s compliance team has to be able to defend every number a tool shows.
  • Who it’s for. Wealth-tech product teams deciding what to build, compliance teams deciding what to allow, AI labs looking for hard, current, exact questions, and advisors asking “can I check this with AI?”
  • The bet. Models are good at language and judgment, and unreliable at rules that change every year. So put the rules in tested code, and let the model choose the inputs and explain the result.

How I work

Seven rules I worked by, each with where it paid off

01

Build the judge before the feature

Every piece started with the check that would prove it right: answer keys before questions, graders before models, fake meetings with answer keys before the meeting app.

The grader for the whole-household decisions passed 12 stand-in checks, including a fake model that answers perfectly and one that lies, before a single real model was asked.

02

The published source decides

When the models and the key disagree, the IRS form, SSA’s manual or the CMS table settles it. Never a vote among models.

Several models from different labs gave the same answer on the new senior deduction, against my key. The IRS form (Schedule 1-A Part V) showed the key was right.

03

Don’t let the builder be the only grader

Claude Code built the engine, so something else checks it: a second engine written by a separate agent that never saw the first; PolicyEngine-US, outside tax software nobody here wrote; a model from another lab (GPT-6 Luna) re-judging wrong-facts replies; and another lab’s model (GPT-6.1 Sol) auditing every published claim.

Where I fell short, said plainly: on the wrong-facts test the lead agent itself re-checked judge labels and changed — of them, under the judge’s written rule and with every change listed. A person spot-checking those labels is still pending.

04

Spend in capped steps

A smoke test under $1, then a small run under $5, then a decision. Every run has a hard spending cap.

The whole November round, — models on two tests, cost — against a $5 cap.

05

Keep a test set nobody tunes on

Some questions are generated from a secret seed, never published and never used to improve anything. They exist only to check that an improvement is real.

The tool redesign was built on public questions, then scored 48 of 50 on held-out questions it had never seen.

06

Cut scope on purpose

Speed comes from saying no. Once the engine proved to be the valuable part, the demo app’s features were frozen.

About 18 of 23 demo commits had gone to polish anyone could build. The next week went to the engine and the proof instead.

07

Every rough edge becomes a test

Use it the way a client would, write down everything that feels wrong, fix it, then save the whole session as a test so it can’t come back.

Two test drives gave 6 notes, then 17. One changed a headline: once the Social Security earnings test was modeled, a “claim at 62” gain of about $149,000 disappeared.

Timeline

Two rounds of building, then an outside audit

Each stage ended with a measured result before the next one started.

  1. Oct 15

    The engine

    A planning engine for federal tax, Social Security, required IRA withdrawals and Medicare premiums, checked for rule errors the same day.

  2. Oct 20

    The first test

    Questions with an answer key I could prove, an outside tax model as referee, and the first models scored. The same day: the Wizard meeting demo, built behind its own grader.

  3. Oct 21

    The calculator, and why it got ignored

    The engine as tools a model can call. Version 1.0 returned only the number and got overruled; 1.1 showed the rule and the form line, and the overruling stopped. Also: a second engine written from the rules alone, and a year-by-year runner for 25-year plans.

  4. Oct 22

    Whole-household decisions, and the connector

    Decisions built so no shortcut works, a grader that tracks every time a model overrules the engine, then the engine inside Claude and ChatGPT, graded by simulated clients before anyone used it.

  5. Nov 14

    The newer, harder set and wrong facts

    An audit of my own first test found the best models had nearly maxed it, and that every fact I gave them was true. So I added harder questions and client files with one planted mistake each.

  6. Nov 15

    An outside audit, and this rewrite

    GPT-6.1 Sol, from a different lab than the agents that built it, checked every published claim against the raw data. It found — errors. They are fixed on this version, and the site was rewritten so the story reads in order.

Mistakes

What the AI got wrong, and what caught it

AI agents did most of the work, so I assumed they would make mistakes and built things to catch them. These are the ones that got caught.

  • A bug in the engine itself. The second engine, written without seeing the first, disagreed on 3 of 2,000 households. One was a real error in the main engine: a 2027 tax threshold. Fixed, then 4,000 of 4,000 agreed.
  • A Medicare premium off by a tier. Income exactly at a threshold was placed one tier too low. Found by a check, fixed, logged.
  • Questions that weren’t fair. Reading the first replies on the wrong-facts test, a model rightly asked about a fact the notes never stated. 14 notes were ambiguous. I fixed them, re-asked, and kept the old replies out of the scores.
  • A spending cap that leaked. One run went $0.39 over its cap because requests already in flight weren’t counted. The cap now reserves each request’s worst case.
  • Grading and wording errors. The outside audit found replies that ran out of room counted as a choice to decline, some judge labels that missed a model naming the mistake, and sentences stronger than the evidence (like “every model was worst at” one kind of error, when two weren’t). All fixed here; the report lists each one.

What’s next

Ranked, with the reason for each

Ordered by what most raises trust in the result per dollar spent.

  1. A CPA signs off on the answer key. Two engines and PolicyEngine agree, but a licensed outside reviewer is what a firm’s compliance team will ask for first. It costs time, not money.
  2. A person spot-checks the judge. On the wrong-facts test, a model decides whether a reply named the planted mistake, and another lab’s model re-judged a quarter of them. A human check on a sample is still owed.
  3. Subtler wrong facts. The planted mistakes are mostly obvious. The next set should look like real intake errors: a balance from last year, a spouse’s benefit entered as the client’s.
  4. Run the 33 held-back whole-household questions. They were never used to build or tune anything, so they show whether the result holds on questions nobody has seen.
  5. Harder compliance questions. Today’s define the issue in the question, so every model scores 91–100%. Useful, but it tests reading, not knowledge.

The demo

Wizard: where the engine meets a meeting

  • What it does. As an advisor and a client talk, the intake form fills itself in. When someone asks a planning question out loud (“what if we converted some of Dana’s IRA?”), the engine’s answer appears as a card a few seconds later, in plain English, with every fact it used and where that fact came from.
  • Graded like everything else. It was built against fake meetings with answer keys, up to an hour long, with hedges, corrections and spouses who disagree.
  • Safe by design. Fake households only. Sensitive numbers are masked in the browser before any model sees them, and speech can be transcribed on the laptop so no audio leaves it.
  • Frozen on purpose. It’s the demo. The engine and the proof are the product.
